For part of the month, you feel like you no longer recognise yourself. Tiredness arrives without warning. A harmless remark brings tears to your eyes. Your belly swells, you crave sugar, you don't want to see anyone. Then your period arrives, and within a few days, you become yourself again.

It's not in your head. It's not a lack of willpower. It's a phase of your cycle, with its own rules. This chapter explains them, simply.

## Your cycle in four phases

A cycle begins on the first day of your period (D1) and ends the day before the next one. It lasts around 28 days on average, but a shorter or longer cycle can be perfectly normal, and it often varies from one month to the next.

It can be divided into four phases:

1. **Your period** (menstrual phase). The body sheds the lining of the uterus. Hormones are at their lowest.
2. **The follicular phase**. Oestrogen rises again, and a follicle gets ready in the ovary. Many women feel more energy and drive.
3. **Ovulation**. The egg is released. It's a short moment, often around the middle of the cycle.
4. **The luteal phase**. It runs from ovulation to the next period. This is the phase this guide is about.

<aside class="encadre">
<p class="encadre-titre">Why "D17–D28"?</p>
<p>In this guide, we talk about D17–D28 (day 17 to day 28) for a 28-day cycle: it's the second half of the luteal phase, when symptoms are felt most. If your cycle lasts 25 or 32 days, shift it: count the <strong>10 to 12 days before your period</strong> instead. What matters is the length, not the day number.</p>
</aside>

## What you may feel, phase by phase

Every woman experiences her cycle in her own way. But some tendencies come up often. Knowing them means no longer being caught off guard.

## The luteal phase: what changes in your body

After ovulation, the follicle that released the egg turns into the **corpus luteum**. It produces **progesterone**, the hormone that prepares the uterus for a possible pregnancy. Oestrogen rises a little, then falls again.

Progesterone has effects you feel directly:

- **Your temperature rises slightly.** You may feel warmer at night and sleep less deeply.
- **Your body holds on to more water.** Hence that swollen feeling, tender breasts, clothes that feel tight.
- **Your digestion slows down.** Bloating, a slower gut: it's common.
- **Your appetite may change.** Wanting to eat more, craving sugar or fat. It's not a lack of discipline.

Then, if there is no pregnancy, the corpus luteum stops. Progesterone and oestrogen **drop** in the last few days before your period. It's often that moment, the descent, that is hardest.

## Why you feel it more strongly than others

Here's what changes everything: in most women who struggle before their period, **hormones are not abnormal**. Their levels look like those of women who feel almost nothing.

What differs is the **sensitivity** of the brain and body to these changes. Cycle hormones talk to the chemical messengers of mood, sleep and appetite. In some women, this conversation is more intense. The moment hormones fall then becomes a moment when everything falls a little: energy, patience, mood.

In other words: **you are not out of balance. You are sensitive to a normal change.** And the good news is that sensitivity can be supported. You can't remove the cycle, but you can change the way you go through it: what you eat, how you move, how you organise your days and how you read your signals. That's what this guide is about.

## What it looks like, in practice

Let's imagine a 28-day cycle. Around D17, all is well. Around D21, you notice you need more sleep and you snack more in the afternoon. Around D24, a meeting that wouldn't have bothered you puts you on edge; in the evening, you don't feel like talking. Around D26, your belly swells and your jeans feel tight. Then your period arrives, and within two or three days, you can breathe again.

If this sounds like you, it's no coincidence. And it's precisely because it is **predictable** that you can **prepare** for it.

## PMS: what are we talking about?

We talk about **premenstrual syndrome (PMS)** when physical or emotional symptoms:

- come back **cycle after cycle**, during the luteal phase;
- **ease in the first days of your period**;
- and **get in the way of your life**: your work, your relationships, your sleep, your energy.

The most common symptoms: tiredness, irritability, sadness or easy tears, anxiety, difficulty concentrating, bloating, sore breasts, headaches, food cravings, sleep problems.

<aside class="encadre encadre-rose">
<p class="encadre-titre">A good habit: write it down</p>
<p>The best way to know whether your symptoms follow your cycle is to note them for two or three cycles. The cycle journal at the end of this guide is made for that. It will also help if you talk to a health professional: you'll arrive with observations, not just impressions.</p>
</aside>

## What this guide does, and what it doesn't

This guide gives you practical lifestyle markers to get through your luteal phase: understanding, nourishing your body, moving the right amount, slowing down without guilt, getting organised.

It makes **no diagnosis**, replaces **no treatment** and promises **no results**. Every body is different: take what speaks to you, leave the rest.

<aside class="encadre encadre-alerte">
<p class="encadre-titre">When to see someone without waiting</p>
<ul>
<li>intense or sudden pain, or pain that stops you from functioning;</li>
<li>very heavy bleeding (a pad or tampon soaked in less than an hour);</li>
<li>fever with pain in your lower abdomen;</li>
<li>a long-delayed period, or a possible pregnancy with pain or bleeding;</li>
<li>new symptoms, or symptoms that get worse from one cycle to the next;</li>
<li>mood so low, anxiety so strong or irritability so intense that it damages your life or your relationships. There is a severe form, <strong>premenstrual dysphoric disorder</strong>, which can be treated.</li>
</ul>
<p>Who to talk to: your midwife, your gynaecologist, your doctor or a psychologist. You don't have to go through this alone. In an emergency: call <strong>15</strong> in France, or your local emergency number.</p>
</aside>
