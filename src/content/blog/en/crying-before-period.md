**Crying more easily, feeling irritable or wanting to be alone in the days before your period is common.** During the [luteal phase](/en/blog/luteal-phase), and then when hormones fall just before your period, many women become more emotionally sensitive. It's not a character flaw, and you're not "unstable". But if these emotions become overwhelming, they deserve to be taken seriously.

## Why am I so emotional before my period?

At the end of the cycle, several things add up:

- **the hormonal changes** of the luteal phase, to which some women are more sensitive than others;
- **tiredness** (see [tiredness before your period](/en/blog/tiredness-before-period)): we cope less well with frustration when we're exhausted;
- **lighter sleep**, which makes everything feel heavier the next day.

The result: a harmless remark brings tears to your eyes, noise becomes unbearable, and you want to escape everyone. Then your period arrives, and within a few days, you feel like yourself again.

## Is it normal to "hate everyone" before your period?

Feeling more irritable at the end of the cycle is common. What matters is the intensity and the impact: if it stays manageable and passes after your period, it's a tendency of your cycle. If it damages your relationships, your work or the way you see yourself, it's not something to handle alone (see the "When to see someone" box).

> What you feel has a logic. It's neither weakness nor a whim.

## What actually helps?

1. **Spot the moment.** Write down your mood every evening for two or three cycles. Knowing "it's D25" changes everything: you stop questioning your whole life on an end-of-cycle evening.
2. **Reduce the load.** Plan quiet evenings and flexible plans for the last few days. Avoid scheduling difficult conversations then.
3. **Look after the basics.** Regular meals, gentle movement, an earlier bedtime: a less tired body copes better with emotions.
4. **Say it.** "I'm at the end of my cycle, I'm more sensitive right now" helps the people around you understand, without having to justify yourself.
5. **Give yourself permission to cry.** Tears aren't a problem to fix. Sometimes, they bring relief.

## What can the people around me do?

Many loved ones want to help without knowing how. Listening without trying to fix, offering rather than imposing, taking a task off your plate, giving you space: that's often all it takes. The Cycle Awakening guide even includes a one-page note to share with him or her.

## When it's no longer "just PMS"

There is a severe form, **premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD)**. Mood is very strongly affected at the end of the cycle: deep sadness, anxiety, anger or despair that stop you from functioning normally. It can be treated. If this sounds like you, talk to your doctor, your midwife, your gynaecologist or a psychologist. Your cycle journal will be valuable for the person listening to you.

You don't have to go through this alone. Understanding your cycle is a first step, and you don't have to take the next ones without help.
